The lecture deliberately presents this as a *method* story (measurement found it, collaboration fixed it, a number measures the fix), not an indictment — module 1 had already introduced the case and promised a return in module 11, so 11.2 delivers the depth and the two numbers. | 2026-09-28 |

Method point the lecture foregrounds over the numbers: the two common benchmarks were skewed toward lighter-skinned subjects, so a **balanced benchmark had to be built before anything could be measured** — the gap pre-existed the study and was invisible because the measuring instrument was unbalanced. | 2026-09-28 |
| Bias in learned representations | Caliskan, Bryson & Narayanan, Science 356(6334) (doi:10.1126/science.aal4230) | Word embeddings learned from web corpora contain human-like association biases, measurable by a systematic method. The lecture states the finding and the fact that it is measurable; it does not reproduce specific effect sizes. | 2026-09-28 |
| **Impossibility — algebraic form** | Chouldechova, Big Data 5(2) (doi:10.1089/big.2016.0047) | The identity the whole lecture is built on: `FPR = [p/(1−p)] · [(1−PPV)/PPV] · TPR`. **Derived in full in 11.3 (three lines) rather than asserted**, then verified numerically against the lab: the check reproduced the lab's FPR to ~1e-17 across four base rates, so students can confirm the algebra themselves. | Error-rate criteria | Hardt, Price & Srebro, arXiv:1610.02413 (page fetched) | Equalized odds and equal opportunity, with the ethical argument for the relaxed form (the graver error is denying a deserving applicant). | 2026-09-28 |
| **Four-fifths rule** | Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, 29 CFR § 1607.4(D) (text verified by search against the regulation) | Added to 11.3 **after** a cross-check found that the course-level post-test (`post-31`) examined this rule while no lecture taught it. Verified wording: a selection rate for any race, sex or ethnic group **less than four-fifths (80%)** of the rate for the highest group "will generally be regarded by the Federal enforcement agencies as evidence of adverse impact", while a greater rate "will generally not be". Both qualifications in the same provision are reproduced in the lecture: smaller differences **may** constitute adverse impact where statistically and practically significant or where applicants were disproportionately discouraged; larger differences **may not** where based on small numbers and not statistically significant, or where special recruiting made the candidate pool atypical. The lecture uses it to make three points — how a fairness criterion becomes a procedure with a numeric threshold, that the 80% figure is a **regulatory choice not a mathematical fact**, and that the result is a screening indicator opening an investigation rather than a finding of discrimination. | 2026-09-29 |

## Notes on what the module deliberately does not claim

- **No EU AI Act application dates in the lectures.** The Commission page gives a staged timetable
  (prohibitions from February 2025 with one from December 2026, transparency obligations from August 2026,
  high-risk obligations from December 2027). These are the most frequently misreported facts in press
  coverage and the timetable has been subject to amendment proposals, so lecture 11.6 teaches the **tier
  structure and its logic** and tells the reader to check dates at the official source. Module 9 separately
  verified the Article 50 transparency date, and nothing here contradicts it.
- **No claim that any specific real system falls in a specific EU risk tier.** Lecture 11.6 and its exercise
  say "التصنيف المرجّح" (the likely classification) and note that the emotion-recognition case falls in the
  prohibited tier "بحسب السياق والصياغة القانونية", because tier assignment is a legal determination about a
  concrete deployment, not something a course can settle.
- **No COMPAS error-rate figures.** The ProPublica/Northpointe dispute is taught for its *structure* — both
  parties correct about different metrics — using the derived identity. Reproducing contested recidivism
  numbers would add nothing to that argument and would import a disputed dataset analysis.
- **No effect sizes from the embeddings study.** Lecture 11.2 states that human-like biases were found in
  learned representations and that they are measurable by a systematic method, without quoting association
  statistics that need the paper's full method to interpret.
- **No claim about Algerian or Maghreb law.** Lecture 11.6 states that most countries in the region have no
  comprehensive statute for these systems *yet*, and builds the practical argument on what holds regardless:
  general law (labour, data protection where it exists, consumer protection, non-discrimination), professional
  responsibility, and institutional policy. No specific national instrument is named or characterised.
- **Labs are synthetic and say so.** `m11_fairness_tradeoff` and the `m11_impossibility` diagram derive every
  reported rate from a perfectly calibrated Beta-distributed score, so the numbers are *forced by the setup*
  rather than chosen — and both state that real scores are not calibrated to begin with, which adds a problem
  rather than removing one. `m11_proxy_bias` generates data from a known process with tunable proxy
  correlation and historical label gap. `m11_explanation_limits` uses a simplified local linear approximation
  and says the instability it shows is shared with the real tools, not unique to the simplification.
- **A deliberate warning against a mathematically available fix.** The fairness lab exposes per-group
  thresholds because equalising error rates generally requires them; both the lab and lecture 11.3 state
  plainly that per-group thresholds are explicit differential treatment and **may be unlawful** in hiring,
  lending and admissions regardless of statistical justification. The module distinguishes what the
  mathematics permits from what the law permits, and locates the gap in governance (11.6).
