# Research log — Module 4: AI vs ML vs DL

## Research log

| Topic | Sources used | Key findings | Date verified |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition of ML | Mitchell (1997), cs.cmu.edu/~tom/mlbook.html | The classic E/T/P formulation forces naming the task, the performance measure and the experience before claiming "learning". | 2026-09-28 |
| Deep learning overview | LeCun, Bengio & Hinton (2015), doi 10.1038/nature14539 | Written by three founders; frames deep learning as layers learning increasingly abstract representations. | 2026-09-28 |
| Reference textbooks | Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville (deeplearningbook.org); James et al. (statlearning.com) | Both are legally free online, which matters for students without library access. ISLR chapter 2 is the clearest intro to splitting and evaluation. | 2026-09-28 |
| Universal approximation | Hornik, Stinchcombe & White (1989), doi 10.1016/0893-6080(89)90020-8 | A single hidden layer with enough units approximates a broad class of functions — an **existence** result that says nothing about how many units, how to find the weights, or how much data is needed. | 2026-09-28 |
| Backpropagation | Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986), doi 10.1038/323533a0 | Chain rule applied backwards; the paper's own framing is "learning representations". | 2026-09-27 |
| Optimizers | Kingma & Ba (arXiv:1412.6980) | Adam adapts a per-parameter learning rate from gradient history. | 2026-09-28 |
| Generalization puzzle | Zhang et al. (arXiv:1611.03530) | Deep nets can fit **random labels** perfectly yet still generalize on real data, so classical capacity arguments alone do not explain generalization. Presented to students as an open research question. | 2026-09-28 |
| Non-deep baselines | Breiman (2001), doi 10.1023/A:1010933404324; Friedman (2001), doi 10.1214/aos/1013203451; Cortes & Vapnik (1995), doi 10.1007/BF00994018 | Random forests, gradient boosting and SVMs remain strong on tabular data — relevant because economics students mostly meet tabular problems. | 2026-09-28 |
| RLHF | Ouyang et al. (arXiv:2203.02155) | Three stages: supervised fine-tuning on instructions, a reward model trained on human preferences, then RL against that reward model. Used to show paradigms **compose**. | 2026-09-28 |
| Metrics in practice | scikit-learn model evaluation docs; Google ML Crash Course | Used to align Arabic terms with the English names students will meet in tools. | 2026-09-28 |
| Target choice and fairness | Obermeyer et al. (2019), doi 10.1126/science.aax2342 | Reused from Module 1 to make the point that choosing the target variable precedes modelling and is an ethical decision. | 2026-09-26 |

## Modern extensions added

- **Self-supervised learning** treated as an *economic* shift (constraint moves from human labelling to compute), not just a technique.
- **RLHF** (Ouyang et al., 2022) as evidence that paradigms compose rather than compete.
- **Foundation models and LLMs** located precisely inside the nesting, preparing Module 7.
- **The open generalization question** (Zhang et al., 2016) presented honestly as unresolved.

## To verify / limitations

- All data in the labs is **generated with numpy** and labelled as illustrative; no real dataset is used or implied.
- `m04_loss_landscape` shows a two-weight surface purely for intuition; the caption states that real models have millions of weights.
- `m04_bias_variance` is a schematic curve, not measured error data.
- The claim that ReLU "eased" deep training is stated qualitatively as one of several practical improvements (per Module 2's three-factor account), without attributing the deep learning boom to it alone.
- Branching claims about which method wins on tabular data are stated as "often competitive, frequently better", not as a universal result, since it depends on dataset size and feature structure.
