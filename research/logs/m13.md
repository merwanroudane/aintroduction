## Research log

| **Ironies of automation** | Bainbridge, Automatica 19(6) (doi:10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8) | The module's spine, and deliberately its oldest source. Four ironies reproduced in 13.2: (1) the designer automates what can be automated, leaving the operator the residue — the hardest tasks; (2) monitoring for a rare event is a task humans fail at structurally, and it gets harder as the system improves; (3) manual skills decay without practice — and it is the same skill review requires; (4) the human is asked to intervene at the worst possible moment (unfamiliar case, little time, out of the loop). Lecture 13.1 uses the 1983 date as a teaching device: the instructor reads irony (1) aloud and asks the class to guess the year. | 2026-09-29 |
| **Automation bias** | Skitka, Mosier & Burdick, IJHCS 51(5) (doi:10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252); design and findings cross-checked by search | **80 participants** in tasks simulating the monitoring and tracking involved in flying a commercial aircraft (location monitoring, holding alignment with a moving target); one group had an automated aid issuing recommendations, the other did not. The group with the aid **missed events the aid did not flag**, performing substantially worse on those events than the group relying on its own knowledge. The omission/commission distinction is taken from this line of work, along with the origin of the term "automation bias". See the note below on the figures deliberately not quoted. | 2026-09-29 |

| Use, misuse, disuse, abuse | Parasuraman & Riley, Human Factors 39(2) (doi:10.1518/001872097778543886) | The classical taxonomy of human–automation relationships. "Misuse" supplies the module's framing that an autonomy level mismatched to the task is itself the defect; "disuse" supplies **automation disuse** as the mirror-image deviation from calibrated trust, so the module warns against both over- and under-reliance. | 2026-09-29 |

| **Calibrated trust** | Lee & See, Human Factors 46(1) (doi:10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392) | Appropriate reliance as the design goal. The module converts this into an explicit correction of a common aim: the objective is **calibrated** trust, not high trust, and it is built by exposing the system's *limits* rather than its capabilities. This is what turns the Limitations field of a model card (11.5) into an operational tool for the reviewer, which 13.4 states directly. | 2026-09-29 |
| **Limits of the human-in-the-loop** | Green & Chen, PACM HCI 3(CSCW) (doi:10.1145/3359152); findings verified by search (ACM page returns 403) | Risk assessments built for **pretrial detention** and **financial lending**, then experiments on how people make predictions with them. Under all conditions participants were **unable to evaluate the accuracy of their own or the risk assessment's predictions**, **did not calibrate their reliance on the risk assessment based on its performance**, and **exhibited bias** in their interactions with it. The authors call for expanding analysis beyond the models to the full sociotechnical context. Lecture 13.1 singles out the second finding as the most damaging, because the entire human-in-the-loop rationale rests on the assumption that the human will trust the system when it deserves trust — which is exactly what failed. | 2026-09-29 |

## Notes on what the module deliberately does not claim

- **No accuracy figures from the automation-bias study.** Secondary summaries report specific accuracy
  percentages for the aided versus unaided groups. Those numbers could only be traced to a secondary
  description, not to the paper's own abstract, so lecture 13.2 states the design (80 participants, flight-like
  monitoring and tracking tasks, aided versus unaided) and the **direction and rough magnitude** of the
  finding ("substantially worse on the events the aid did not flag") without quoting percentages. The
  omission/commission taxonomy, which is what the lecture actually teaches from, is stable across sources.
- **The six autonomy levels are a teaching construction, not a quoted standard.** Lecture 13.3 builds a
  six-rung ladder (no assistance, suggest, ranked recommendation, execute on approval, execute unless vetoed,
  full autonomy) on the foundation of Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens and with SAE J3016 as a worked example
  of good grading practice. The lecture does not attribute this specific six-level wording to either source,
  and the metadata and glossary describe it as the module's ladder. The two additions the module makes its
  own teaching points — the **ranking effect** at level 2 and the **two failure conditions of the veto
  window** at level 4 — are presented as reasoning from the cited literature, not as findings from it.
- **No claim that any real system sits at a particular level.** Every exercise says "the likely level" and
  distinguishes the **declared** level from the **actual** level, because the actual level is an empirical
  question answered by the measurements in 13.2, not by reading a policy document.
- **The vigilance lab's numbers are illustrative and say so.** `m13_vigilance` models detection probability as
  a saturating function of how many genuine errors a reviewer encounters, which is a **stated assumption**
  chosen to expose the mechanism, not a measured relationship. The caption and the expander both say the
  *trend* is what the human-factors literature establishes and the absolute values are not measured from any
  system. The lab deliberately reports **both** directions — the share of missed errors rises as the system
  improves while the count falls — so students do not take away a false claim that better systems cause more
  total harm; the argument made instead is that each escaping error now lands in a system everyone trusts.
  The monotonicity of the relationship was checked across the full slider range before the defaults were set.
- **The rubber-stamp detector's thresholds are proposed for teaching, not standards.** The lab says so
  explicitly and makes the point that the student's job is to set and write down their **own** threshold,
  because an unwritten threshold produces no action — tying back to 11.6. The lab also warns against reading
  any single indicator alone, and states that a very high override rate is not automatically good either
  (it may mean the system should not be deployed at that autonomy level, or that reviewers are overriding
  without examining), which is why the documented-justification share is read alongside it.
- **No legal or disciplinary conclusions about individual responsibility.** Lecture 13.1 argues that blaming
  an individual for a procedure that made examination impossible is itself a symptom of the defect, and the
  instructor note tells teachers to say this plainly so the module is not heard as blaming reviewers. It does
  not make claims about how liability is actually allocated in any jurisdiction.
