### Note on the fabricated-citation case (lecture 8.5)

The case is described at the level that is documented and stable: in 2023, US lawyers submitted a brief
containing case citations produced with a chat assistant; the cases did not exist; the fabrications passed
review because they were **formally consistent** (correct citation format, real courts, plausible years);
and when the assistant was asked whether the cases were real, it said yes.

The lecture deliberately does **not** name parties, docket numbers or sanction amounts. Those details are
not needed for the teaching point, and reproducing them from memory would be exactly the kind of unverified
specificity the module warns against. The pedagogical point is the missing step — opening a case database
and searching each citation by name.

### Note on detection tools (lecture 8.6)

The lecture makes four claims about AI-text detectors: false positives, false negatives on lightly edited
text, absence of an auditable criterion, and an arms race. These are stated as **structural** limitations
that follow from how the tools work, and the lecture draws a normative conclusion (a detector score is not
evidence) rather than a quantitative one. No accuracy figure is cited, because published figures vary by
tool, version and text type, and would date immediately. The bias direction — against simple, well-organised
writing and against non-native writers — is stated because it follows from what the detectors measure
(statistical regularity of text), and because the harm of a false accusation is asymmetric.
